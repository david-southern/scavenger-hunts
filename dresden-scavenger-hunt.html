📍 Theaterplatz
The King on Horseback
Up to 35 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

You're standing in Theaterplatz — the grand square where Dresden's most famous landmarks cluster together. Find the equestrian statue in the middle of the square. This is King John, a mid-19th-century ruler who recognized and preserved Saxon culture and paid for the opera house behind the statue. Now face the Semperoper and look at the two figures flanking its entrance — these are the two greatest figures in German literary culture, welcoming all who enter. To your far left is a smaller Neoclassical building called the Schinkelwache. It was designed in a Prussian Classicist style by the architect Karl Friedrich Schinkel — which is why it looks out of place among the Baroque buildings around it. Today it serves as a popular café and the opera's box office.

💡 Did You Know?

Three opera houses have stood on this exact spot. The first was destroyed by fire in 1869. The second was destroyed by firebombs in 1945. The rebuilt Semperoper — named after its architect Gottfried Semper — continues to be a world-class venue. Tickets for performances go on sale a full year in advance and are very hard to come by. Look closely at the stonework on the buildings around you — it appears very sooty and dark. This is not from pollution or firebombing, as many visitors assume. It is caused by natural oxidation: the local sandstone simply turns black in about 50 years.

📍 Between the Semperoper & Zwinger
The Composer Who Bookended History
Up to 35 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

Head to the gap between the Semperoper and the Zwinger. Standing in front of the Alte Meister Café you'll find a bronze statue of a composer named Carl Maria von Weber. His opera Der Freischütz holds a remarkable distinction: it was the very last opera performed in the Semperoper before the building was destroyed in 1945 — and the very first opera performed when the rebuilt Semperoper reopened in 1985. From here, go up the stairs and turn left up the ramp. As you climb what was once a rampart, notice the faint echo of the moat to your right. The opera house is named the Semperoper after its architect Gottfried Semper — the same man who later added one of the wings of the Zwinger you're about to enter.

💡 Did You Know?

At the top of the ramp, past the adorable fountain, you'll reach a balcony with a breathtaking view of the Zwinger courtyard below. If you visit the Old Masters Gallery inside the Zwinger, you can find Bernardo "Canaletto" Bellotto's 1752 painting Zwinger Courtyard in Dresden — which shows this exact balcony view as it looked over 270 years ago. The ramp you're walking up was once part of the city's defensive fortifications.

📍 The Zwinger
The Party Palace & Its Museums
Up to 60 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

From the balcony, descend the small stairs to the Nymphs' Bath — a fountain grotto where 18th-century aristocrats relaxed among cascading waterfalls. Cross through the orangery into the middle of the courtyard. You can see all four wings: To your right, the Rampart Pavilion (Wallpavillon), topped by a figure of Hercules hoisting the earth — with the features of Augustus the Strong himself, making him the ultimate symbol of royal strength. To its left, the Crown Gate (Kronentor), topped by four golden eagles supporting a crown — symbolizing Polish royalty, since Augustus was also King of Poland. Next, the Glockenspiel Pavilion, with 40 bells made of Meissen porcelain near the top (they chime every 15 minutes and play a 3-minute melody at 10:15, 14:15, and 18:15). And finally, the stern Semper Gallery — added 100 years after the rest by Gottfried Semper.

💡 Did You Know?

The Zwinger houses three museums: the Old Masters Gallery in the Semper Gallery wing, the Porcelain Collection in the Glockenspiel Pavilion wing, and the Mathematics-Physics Salon in the Rampart Pavilion wing. The word "Zwinger" refers to the no-man's-land between the outer and inner city walls — and although it looks like a palace, no one ever lived here. It was built solely for royal celebrations. Anticipating WWII bombs, Dresdeners hid the art treasures in underground mines and cellars in the countryside. This saved them from Allied bombs — but not from the Soviet army, who took the art to Moscow. It was returned after Stalin's death in 1953.

📍 Taschenberg & Sky Bridge
The Mistress's Mansion
Up to 30 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

Exit the Zwinger through the Glockenspiel Pavilion. Cross the street and the tram tracks, turn left, then curve right onto the street called Taschenberg. The bright yellow building on your right is the Taschenberg Palace — built for Augustus the Strong's mistress, and later home to the crown prince. It was ruined until 1990, when it was restored and became a five-star hotel. Look ahead for a yellow-windowed sky bridge — it connects the Taschenberg to the Royal Palace, so royalty could walk between them without having to deal with the public below. Walk under it. Ahead and to the right is a blocky modern building with a large exterior mural — the Palace of Culture (Kulturpalast), built by the communist government in 1969.

💡 Did You Know?

The Palace of Culture mural depicts communist themes — workers, strong women, well-cared-for elders, teachers and students, the red star, and the seal of the former East Germany. Little of this propagandist art survives in post-communist Germany; what does remain, like this mural, is protected. The refurbished concert hall inside the Palace of Culture is today home to the Dresden Philharmonic.

📍 Royal Palace (Residenzschloss)
The Green Vault Treasuries
Up to 40 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

Walk along Schlossstrasse with the Royal Palace on your left. The gate on your left is one of the palace entrances, with the Watchman's Tower above it. Inside you'll find the Green Vault (Grünes Gewölbe) treasuries — Augustus the Strong's legendary collection. The palace also has a clock tower. Pause here and appreciate something: the car-free city center and the tram system mean you can walk and look around without dodging traffic.

💡 Did You Know?

The Wettin dynasty ruled Saxony for more than 800 years, right up until the end of World War I — just like the Romanovs in Russia and the Habsburgs in Austria. The prince-elector of Saxony was one of Germany's most powerful people — one of a small group of nobles who elected the Holy Roman Emperor. Augustus the Strong was both prince-elector of Saxony and king of Poland. He was unlucky at war but a clever diplomat and a lover of the arts — and it is thanks to him and the Wettin rulers that Dresden has its extraordinary architectural and artistic heritage.

📍 Katholische Hofkirche
The Catholic Cathedral in a Protestant City
Up to 30 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

Continue to Palace Square. To your left is the Katholische Hofkirche with its distinctive green-copper, onion-domed steeple. Look for the elevated passage connecting the Hofkirche directly to the Royal Palace — designed so royalty could attend church without the hassle of dealing with the public. Augustus the Strong pragmatically converted from Saxon Protestantism to Catholicism in order to become King of Poland. The result: a Catholic church, built for a converted Protestant ruler, in a predominantly Protestant city. You'll notice the city center features both the Protestant Frauenkirche (ahead) and this large Catholic Hofkirche — because Augustus was "finessing it both ways," keeping both his Saxon Protestant subjects and his Polish Catholic subjects content.

💡 Did You Know?

Augustus the Strong was unlucky at war but a clever diplomat. He pragmatically converted from his Saxon Protestantism to a more Polish-friendly Catholicism to secure the Polish crown — becoming King Augustus II of Poland. His conversion meant he needed to maintain both a grand Protestant church (the Frauenkirche) for his Saxon subjects, and a grand Catholic church (the Hofkirche) for his Polish identity. The elevated walkway between the church and palace let him attend Mass privately, above the heads of the ordinary people below.

📍 Augustusstraße
The World's Longest Porcelain Mural
Up to 50 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

Turn around to face the palace gate you came through and look left. You'll see the long mural called the Parade of Princes (Fürstenzug) — painted on 24,000 tiles of Meissen porcelain and longer than a football field. Walk from the end nearest the Hofkirche. The very last figure in the procession (the first you'll see from this direction) is the artist himself, Wilhelm Walther. In front of him come commoners — miners, farmers, carpenters, teachers, students, artists — and then the royals. Find Augustus the Strong (look for the year 1694 marked above him): his horse stomps on a rose, the symbol of Martin Luther and the Protestant movement — representing the faith he gave up to gain the Polish crown. At the very front of the parade: an announcer, a band, and 12th-century cheerleaders, heralding the arrival of the wondrous procession.

💡 Did You Know?

The porcelain tiles are originals from 1907. They were fired three times at 2,400 degrees Fahrenheit when created — and then fired again during the 1945 firestorm, which only reached 1,800 degrees. Because they had already withstood a higher temperature, the tiles survived. The mural was created to commemorate Saxon history after Saxony became part of Germany in 1871. The artist carefully studied armor and clothing through the ages, accurately tracing seven centuries of fashion and weaponry. These tiles are among the very few original artworks in Dresden's Old Town that did not need to be rebuilt after the war.

📍 The Tournament Yard & Neumarkt
Where Knights Jousted & a Reformer Stands
Up to 25 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

Just past the mural, take a detour sharply right into a long, narrow courtyard. Many of those featured in the mural may have put on their ceremonial armor and jousted right here. Look at the long white colonnade — imagine wooden stands filling it, packed with cheering spectators. Look left for the pool where the horses cooled down before being stabled. Continue through into the big square where a statue of Martin Luther stands. He is holding the Bible in German — which he personally translated from Hebrew and ancient Greek so that regular people could wrestle with the Word of God directly. When translating, Luther used colloquial, everyday German, forming the basis for what is now considered "High German." So besides being the Great Reformer, Luther is considered the father of the modern German language. The statue was toppled in 1945 but has been cleaned up and is back on his feet again.

💡 Did You Know?

The square beyond the tournament yard is Neumarkt — once a central square ringed by the homes of rich merchants, now alive again with people and cafés. The towering church behind Luther's statue is the Frauenkirche — your next stop. Luther came from this part of Germany and spent most of his life in nearby Erfurt and Wittenberg. His decision to translate the Bible into the language ordinary Germans actually spoke — rather than Latin — was the spark that ignited the Protestant Reformation and changed European history.

📍 Neumarkt & Frauenkirche
The Church That Rose from the Rubble
Up to 50 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

The Frauenkirche is the symbol and soul of Dresden. When completed in 1743, it was Germany's tallest Protestant church at 310 feet. Its unique central-stone-cupola design gave it the nickname "the stone bell." On the night of February 13, 1945, firebombs fell. The church smoldered for two days before finally collapsing. It was left as a rubble pile and turned into a deliberate peace monument — a reminder of the destruction of war — for decades. Only after German reunification was the decision made to rebuild it completely, and it reopened in 2005. Circle around to the river side of the church (near door E) to find a preserved piece of the original bombed rubble, mounted with a small metal relief showing exactly where that piece came from. Look closely at the church exterior — you can spot the darker, fire-scorched original stones among the paler new sandstone. Builders matched them back to their original positions using old photographs.

💡 Did You Know?

Nearby, look for the dome nicknamed "the lemon juicer" — it caps the exhibition hall of the Academy of Fine Arts. The grand Neo-Renaissance building to the right of the square is the Albertinum, originally the Saxon armory, rebuilt in the 1880s, and today an art gallery with the city's best collection of 19th- and 20th-century art. After the walk, climb the steps at the end of the square to the ramparts — now a park — for a view toward the Elbe River.

📍 Brühlsche Terrasse
The Balcony of Europe
Up to 35 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

Walk back toward the Hofkirche and Royal Palace to reach the Brühlsche Terrasse — a promenade that was once a defensive rampart. Look along the side of the terrace facing the Elbe River to see the openings where cannons once pointed. It was given as a reward to a Saxon minister named Brühl, who distinguished himself as a tax collector for Frederick Augustus II. In the early 1800s it became a public park with a leafy canopy of linden trees, earning the nickname "The Balcony of Europe." Look out at the Elbe. To the left in the distance, the hills are home to vineyards producing some of Germany's northernmost wine — mostly white, consumed almost entirely by locals because only a small amount of land is suitable for growing.

💡 Did You Know?

Dresden claims to have the world's largest and oldest fleet of historic paddleboat steamers — nine riverboats from the 19th century, still sailing today. Look out at the Elbe to spot them. During massive floods in August 2002, the water reached nearly to the top of the bridge arches below. At the far end of the terrasse, stop at the grove of linden trees with a fountain at the center — from here you'll look out toward the Augustus Bridge and the New Town beyond.

📍 Augustusbrücke & The Golden Rider
The Grand Finale
Up to 30 points
▼
🔍 Your Mission

At the far end of the Brühlsche Terrasse, stop at the grove of linden trees with a fountain at the center and look out at the Augustus Bridge (Augustusbrücke). It has connected Dresden's old and new towns since 1319, when it was the first stone bridge over the Elbe here. Walk across it. At the far end you'll find a gleaming golden equestrian statue — a symbol of the entire city. This is Augustus the Strong, nicknamed the "Golden Rider" (Goldene Reiter). Beyond him rises the spire of the Three Kings Church. Turn around and look back at the Old Town skyline — you've now visited the history behind everything you can see.

💡 Did You Know?

During massive floods in August 2002, the water on the Elbe reached nearly to the top of the bridge's arches. The Augustus Bridge has stood in some form on this spot since 1319 — when it was the very first stone bridge over the Elbe at this location. The New Town on this side of the river is a great area for evening exploring or dining after your walk.
