Welcome to Den Haag

More than just a capital

The Dutch constitution may identify Amsterdam as the official "capital," but The Hague has been the Netherlands' seat of government since 1588. It's home to the country's parliament and to the International Court of Justice — where nations try to settle their disputes without bloodshed. It's also packed with extraordinary art, mind-bending museums, and a beloved beach that generations of Dutch families return to every summer.

Mauritshuis

Dutch Golden Age Masterpieces

One of the Netherlands' best art museums, the Mauritshuis features Dutch golden age art by Vermeer, Rembrandt, Rubens, and many others. This so-called "mini-Rijksmuseum" is more intimate and less overwhelming than its bigger cousin in Amsterdam — and well worth a visit.

💡 Family Tip: Grab the free audio guide and let everyone pick their own favourite painting. Meet at the café to compare — you'll be surprised who ends up loving what.
🧭 How do I navigate the museum? ▾
The entrance to the permanent collection is to the left as you enter the main door. Climb two flights of stairs. Floor 1 has a few highlights — skip it if you're short on time. Floor 2 (actually the fourth floor) is packed with masterpieces and is the one you don't want to miss.
🖼️ Room-by-Room Highlights — tap to open
Rm 2–3Rubens: Old Woman & a Boy with Candles▾
Begin in Room 2 with portraits of wealthy patrons by Peter Paul Rubens and his protégé Anthony van Dyck. In Room 3, Rubens' touching scene (c. 1616–17) features an elderly woman passing her light to a boy — encouraging him to enjoy life in a way she perhaps hasn't. Her serene smile suggests hope that he won't have the same regrets she does. Rubens was an early Dutch adopter of the ultrarealism practiced by the Italian painter Caravaggio.
Rm 4–8Holbein: Portrait of Robert Cheseman▾
Work through Rooms 4, 5, 6, and 7 to find Hans Holbein the Younger's Portrait of Robert Cheseman (1533) — the chief falconer of England's King Henry VIII. Complete your circuit in Room 8, then head up the stairs to Floor 2.
Rm 9Rembrandt: The Anatomy Lesson▾
The Anatomy Lesson of Dr. Nicolaes Tulp (1632) made Rembrandt a star in Amsterdam. This physician really did dissect a human cadaver once each year to better understand anatomy — for example, how muscles and tendons allow a hand to open and close. Surgeons huddled close to learn, and the public was also invited. Notice Rembrandt's uniquely engaging version of a group portrait: inquisitive faces lean in, hanging on the doctor's every word, with each surgeon's face carrying a different expression. The cadaver resembles a notorious criminal of the day.
Rm 10Rembrandt: Self-Portrait & Two African Men▾
Two African Men (1661) features a pair who may have been brothers — or possibly neighbors of the artist, who lived near a small community of free Black people in Amsterdam. The Rembrandt Self-Portrait (1669) is the last of more than 80 he painted throughout his life. The clothes are painted lightly, but the face is built up layer by layer. We see a man beaten down by life: bankrupt, having lost both great loves of his life and his only child. Rembrandt would be dead within a year. This room also features three portraits by Frans Hals, whose subjects look resplendent in lace and ruffs.
Rm 12Paulus Potter: The Bull▾
Paulus Potter's The Bull (1647) was once one of the Mauritshuis' most beloved works. It elevates livestock to high art — celebrating this bull as if he's a ruff-collared aristocrat. Big, dreamy, brown eyes; mouth open as if to speak knowledgeably; legs spread in a dynamic stance. The cow, goat, sheep, and even the farmer are an afterthought to the painting's star. There's even a perfectly formed patty of manure in the foreground. Remember this pose — craning his neck slightly and glancing over his shoulder. You'll see a similar one very soon.
Rm 14Fabritius: The Goldfinch & Girl Eating Oysters▾
Carel Fabritius' The Goldfinch (c. 1654) — made famous by Donna Tartt's 2013 novel of the same title — shows a pet goldfinch perched on its feeder, shackled by a slender chain. Fabritius was Rembrandt's pupil and Vermeer's teacher. Get close to see how the artist rendered the wing by painting bold yellow strokes over black and then scratching the paint off with the back of his brush. Jan Steen's Girl Eating Oysters (c. 1658–1660) is loaded with 17th-century innuendo: oysters were considered a powerful aphrodisiac, and a bed peeks through the curtains behind the grinning subject.
Rm 15Vermeer: Girl with a Pearl Earring▾
Sometimes called "the Dutch Mona Lisa," Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring (c. 1665) is a "tronie" — a type of picture in which the painter's goal is not to depict an individual person but to capture a mood or character. We don't even know who this mysterious girl is. Wearing a blue turban and with a gigantic pearl dangling from her earlobe, she glances over her shoulder and catches the viewer's gaze. Look closely: the famous pearl is essentially formed by two simple brushstrokes. Does the pose seem familiar? It's Potter's bull. On the opposite wall, Vermeer's View of Delft (c. 1660–1661) uses a cloudy-to-sunny contrast to draw your eye deep into the canvas.
Rm 16Frans Hals: Laughing Boy▾
Look near the windows for Frans Hals' lovable Laughing Boy (c. 1625) — an exuberant scamp grinning widely despite his decaying teeth and rat's-nest hair. Like Girl with a Pearl Earring, this is another tronie — less about the subject than about what he's doing: laughing spontaneously. A perfect final note for your tour.

Binnenhof

Seat of Dutch Political Power

The castle-like Binnenhof complex, overlooking Hofvijver lake right in the center of The Hague, is the seat of Dutch political power. The prime minister's office is here, and it's also the meeting place of the two-house parliament, the Staten-Generaal. The complex is undergoing an ambitious, multi-year renovation — check ahead for open sections.

💡 Family Tip: If you can reach the inner courtyard, look for the golden fountain depicting Queen Beatrix — a reminder that Dutch parliamentarians govern with the monarch's symbolic approval. The stroll along Hofvijver lake is always open and worth a look.
🏰 What is the Knights' Hall? ▾
Dominating the middle of the complex is the historic Knights' Hall (Ridderzaal), where the two houses of parliament meet jointly on special occasions — including the annual opening of parliament by the monarch.
🗳️ How does Dutch parliament work? ▾
The power resides in the directly elected Second Chamber — like a House of Representatives. The mostly figurehead First Chamber is more like the UK's House of Lords and meets once weekly. Both chambers meet jointly in the Knights' Hall on special occasions.
🌊 What is Hofvijver lake? ▾
The Hofvijver lake sits right alongside the Binnenhof and the Mauritshuis. If you have time and nice weather, it's enjoyable to stroll the manicured, tree-lined trail all the way around — passing various other museums along the way.

Escher in the Palace

Mind-Bending Optical Art

Celebrating Dutch optical illusionist M.C. Escher (1898–1972), this thoughtful exhibit is crowd-pleasing, supremely engaging, and deeper than you might expect — much like the artist's works. The building itself — the former winter palace of Queen Mother Emma, with far-out chandeliers by Dutch artist Hans van Bentem — is also worth a look. Entry: €12.50. Open Tuesday–Sunday, 11:00–17:00.

💡 Family Tip: The top floor features displays that let you step right into an Escher engraving. A free digital audio guide is available for your phone. The mandatory bag check in the basement is free.
🎨 What will I see at the exhibit? ▾
The exhibit traces both the artist's life and his artistic evolution — covering perspective, reflections, metamorphoses, impossible architecture, and tessellations (infinitely repeating patterns of tiles, which he became obsessed with after visiting the Alhambra in Granada). One of his "metamorphosis" works is mounted in a giant circle, allowing you to walk around it and see how it truly is an infinite loop that works in both directions. You can also watch a 20-minute film about the artist.
🔢 Why did mathematicians love Escher before art critics did? ▾
Escher may not be considered a "serious artist" by art-world elites, but the exhibit does an admirable job of challenging that assumption. His work was embraced by scientists and mathematicians who appreciated the precise logic behind his impossible worlds — years before a wider audience caught on.

Peace Palace

International Court of Justice

The palace houses the International Court of Justice and the Permanent Court of Arbitration. These two courts attempt to reach amicable settlements for international disagreements, such as border disputes. While the judicial process is interesting, the building itself is the big draw. A free visitors center offers multimedia exhibits about the building and the courts.

💡 Family Tip: The visitors center has a persuasive video about the history of the Peace Palace and the role of international law — a great conversation starter about how nations try to resolve disputes without going to war.
🏛️ Who built the Peace Palace — and why? ▾
The Peace Palace is a gift from American industrialist Andrew Carnegie. It's filled with opulent decorations donated by grateful nations who found diplomatic peace here — from exquisite Japanese tapestries, to a Hungarian tile fountain, to French inlay floors.
⚖️ What do the two courts actually do? ▾
The International Court of Justice and the Permanent Court of Arbitration both work to settle disputes between nations without bloodshed. You'll learn how modern nations attempt to resolve their differences here instead of on the battlefield — a powerful idea to explore with your family.

Scheveningen

The Dutch Coney Island

This Dutch Coney Island, with its broad sandy beach, is at its liveliest on sunny summer afternoons — but its biggest appeal is watching urbanites from all over South Holland enjoy a day at the seashore. Dominating the scene is the long double-decker pleasure pier: shops down below, a boardwalk up top, and a bungee-jumping pavilion at the far end. A café-lined promenade stretches along the sand.

💡 Family Tip: Try pronouncing "Scheveningen" (roughly SKHEH-veh-ning-ehn). In WWII, Dutch soldiers used it to detect German spies — the guttural "sch" sound is nearly impossible for non-Dutch speakers. See who in your family can nail it!
🕵️ How did a beach town name catch WWII spies? ▾
Dutch soldiers would quiz suspicious-looking visitors on how to pronounce "Scheveningen" to determine who was Dutch-born and ferret out potential German spies. This is the textbook example of an insider passphrase, known as a "shibboleth" — a word used to identify outsiders who cannot pronounce it correctly.

Escher in the Palace — A Closer Look

M.C. Escher: The Man Behind the Impossible Worlds

The exhibit traces both Escher's life and his artistic evolution. As a young man, Escher fell in love with Italy, and his perspective-driven works built a foundation that served him well later in life. His art eventually shifted from landscapes of his beloved Italy to what have been termed "mindscapes" — fantastical, impossible worlds.

His Life & Journey

Maurits Cornelis Escher was born on June 17, 1898 in Leeuwarden, the Netherlands. His father was a civil engineer — one of eight Dutch engineers invited by the Japanese Emperor to modernize Japan's water infrastructure. Though his parents hoped he'd become an architect, a teacher at art school in Haarlem recognized his graphic talent and redirected him toward printmaking.

As a young man Escher fell deeply in love with Italy, and his perspective-driven works — of everything from jagged coastlines to St. Peter's Basilica — built a visual foundation that served him throughout his life. During travels to Spain in 1922 and again in 1936, he visited the Alhambra in Granada and became obsessed with the Moorish tile patterns: infinitely repeating shapes that locked together without gaps.

The rise of Mussolini's fascism led to Escher's departure from Italy with his family, and an itinerant period as he moved from place to place before finally settling in the Dutch town of Baarn. Here Escher synthesized his various artistic obsessions: perspective, reflections, metamorphoses, impossible architecture, and tessellations. His art shifted from the real landscapes of Italy to "mindscapes" — fantastical, impossible worlds.

He became internationally famous in the 1950s after his work gained wider attention. In 1955 he received a knighthood in the Order of Orange-Nassau. He completed his last woodcut, Snakes, in 1969 and passed away on March 27, 1972.

Trivia & Surprises

Did You Know?

Extra stories from The Hague — things that deserve a closer look. Tap to reveal each answer.

🐟
Why did 17th-century Dutch painters turn everyday subjects — bulls, goldfinches, oysters — into serious art?
The Dutch golden age was driven by a prosperous merchant class rather than royal or church patrons. These new patrons wanted paintings of their world — ships, food, animals, their own faces. This created a revolutionary naturalism in art: the idea that the everyday world was worthy of beauty and serious artistic attention. You can see this spirit in nearly every room of the Mauritshuis.
👁️
What is a "tronie" — and why does it matter for understanding the Mauritshuis?
A tronie is a type of Dutch golden age painting where the goal is not to depict a specific person, but to capture a mood, expression, or character type. Several of the Mauritshuis' most famous works are tronies — including Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring and Frans Hals' Laughing Boy. This is why we don't know who the girl in the pearl earring painting actually is — she may never have been intended as a portrait of a real individual at all.
🎓
Carel Fabritius painted The Goldfinch. What connects him to both Rembrandt and Vermeer?
Fabritius was Rembrandt's pupil and Vermeer's teacher — making him the artistic bridge between two of the greatest Dutch masters. The Goldfinch shows traces of both: Rembrandt's rich layering and Vermeer's luminous simplicity. Tragically, Fabritius died young in the Delft gunpowder explosion of 1654, the same year he painted The Goldfinch, and very little of his work survives.
🕊️
The Peace Palace was completed in 1913. What happened just one year later that tested everything it stood for?
World War I broke out in 1914 — just one year after the Peace Palace opened its doors. The war was a sobering reminder of how hard peace is to keep. The palace survived the war and has gone on to house the International Court of Justice, which continues to work toward peaceful resolution of international disputes today.
🐂
Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring shares a secret pose with a painting of a bull. What connects them?
In Room 12, Paulus Potter's bull cranes his neck and glances back over one shoulder — the same pose as Vermeer's girl in Room 15. Both paintings are tronies — not about recording a specific subject, but capturing a fleeting expression and character. Even a bull gets treated as if he were a dignified aristocrat worthy of a formal portrait.
🏛️
Amsterdam is the official capital of the Netherlands — so why is The Hague the real center of power?
The Dutch constitution names Amsterdam as the capital, but The Hague has been the seat of government since 1588 — over 400 years. Parliament, the prime minister's office, and the International Court of Justice are all here. Amsterdam is the cultural and commercial capital; The Hague is where the governing actually happens.
