Top Sites for Families

Berlin is a sprawling city packed with history at every corner. These are the must-see spots — dramatic, thought-provoking, and fascinating for the whole family, bringing to life the stories of ordinary people who lived through extraordinary times.

The Reichstag

Germany's parliament building has witnessed the fall of a kaiser, the rise of Hitler, and the reunification of a nation. British architect Norman Foster added its iconic glass dome in 1999 — a symbol of democracy reborn. The dome rises 155 feet and lets visitors spiral to the top for sweeping city views.

🔥 What's the Reichstag fire — and why does it matter? ▾
In 1933, the Reichstag building nearly burned down. Many historians believe Hitler planned the fire as an excuse to blame communists and seize emergency powers. Whether he did or not, Hitler used the fire to end real democracy in Germany. Within months he was Führer — absolute ruler — and the Reichstag was barely used for another 60 years.
🪟 Why is the dome made of glass? ▾
The glass dome is a deliberate symbol: the German people can literally look down into their parliament at work. It represents transparency in government — a direct reaction to the secrecy of the Nazi era and communist East Germany. The 360 mirrors also bounce natural sunlight into the legislative chamber below.
🏳️ What happened at the Reichstag at the end of World War II? ▾
As Soviet troops closed in on Berlin in 1945, some 1,500 German soldiers made their last stand right here at the Reichstag. The famous photo of Soviet soldiers raising the Red Flag on the roof became one of the most iconic images of World War II. You can still see shell damage on the building's exterior walls.

Berlin Wall Victims Memorial

A row of white crosses stands along Ebertstrasse, right where the Berlin Wall once ran north to south, dividing the city. This side was democratic West Berlin; the other side, Soviet-controlled East Berlin. Each cross commemorates one of the many brave East Berliners who died trying to cross the Wall to freedom.

✝️ Read the Names: The last person killed here was 20-year-old Chris Gueffroy, shot through the heart — just nine months before the Wall fell. Fresh roses are often left at his cross. A thin strip of memorial bricks embedded in the street pavement marks exactly where the Wall stood.
🧱 How close was the Wall to the Reichstag? ▾
The Berlin Wall ran right behind the Reichstag — Germany's parliament building sat just feet from the Iron Curtain. The Reichstag fell into disuse during the Cold War because it was so close to the Wall. West Germany even moved its capital to the remote city of Bonn during this period, partly because divided Berlin felt too exposed and unstable.
🌹 Are there other memorials nearby? ▾
Yes — several memorials dedicated to other groups targeted by the Nazis are located in the same vicinity, including memorials to the Sinti and Roma people and to gay people persecuted under the Nazi regime. The area around the Reichstag and Brandenburg Gate is one of the densest concentrations of memorial sites anywhere in the world.

Memorial to Politicians Who Opposed Hitler

Just outside the Reichstag entrance, a row of 96 slate slabs rises from the ground — they look almost like a bike rack. These honor the 96 Reichstag members who had the courage to speak out against Adolf Hitler and the rising tide of fascism. For their bravery, they were arrested, persecuted, and ultimately murdered.

📋 Look Closely: Each slab shows a name, political party — most are KPD (Communists) or SPD (Social Democrats) — and the date and location of death. The letters "KZ" on a slab mean that person died in a concentration camp.
🗳️ How did Hitler come to power if people voted against him? ▾
Hitler's Nazi party never won a majority in a fair election — they reached about 37% at their peak. But after the Reichstag fire of 1933, Hitler used emergency powers to suspend civil liberties, arrest opponents, and intimidate the remaining members into passing an "Enabling Act" that gave him dictatorial control. The politicians honored by these slabs were among those who voted against that act — knowing the danger they faced.
💬 Why does this memorial matter today? ▾
These 96 people show that resistance was possible even in the darkest times. They chose to speak out when silence would have been safer. Germany honors them here — right next to the parliament building — as a reminder that democracy requires active defenders, and that courage in the face of tyranny is something worth remembering and celebrating.

Brandenburg Gate

The grandest of Berlin's original 14 city gates, this massive monument became the symbol of a divided city — stranded in no-man's-land between East and West from 1961 to 1989. On November 9, 1989, jubilant Berliners stood atop the Wall right here, celebrating their freedom with flowers and hammers.

🕊️ Think About This: For 28 years, German families were separated — some on one side of this gate, some on the other — and could not visit each other. Imagine not being able to see your grandparents or cousins for your entire childhood.
🐴 What's the statue on top — and why did Napoleon steal it? ▾
The four-horse chariot on top is driven by the Goddess of Peace (later renamed Goddess of Victory). When Napoleon conquered Prussia in 1806, he carted the statue off to the Louvre in Paris as a war trophy. When the Prussians defeated Napoleon and took it back in 1813, they renamed it to celebrate their triumph!
🎤 What famous American speech was given near here? ▾
In 1987, US President Ronald Reagan stood in front of the Brandenburg Gate (on the Western side) and famously challenged Soviet leader Gorbachev: "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!" Two years later — the Wall actually came down. Earlier, in 1963, President Kennedy had declared "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner) in solidarity with West Berliners.
Memorial tap to expand

Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe

A field of 2,711 gray concrete pillars of varying heights covers an entire city block. Designed by Jewish-American architect Peter Eisenman and completed in 2005, this was the first formal German-government-sponsored Holocaust memorial. The underground information center tells the devastating stories of real victims and families.

💭 Walk Through: As you walk between the pillars, the ground slopes and the columns grow taller until you feel surrounded and disoriented. That feeling is intentional — it gives a small sense of the isolation and fear Holocaust victims felt.
✡️ Why was the word "murdered" in the title such a big deal? ▾
Using the word "murdered" was a profound statement — Germany, as a nation, was officially admitting to a crime, not softening it with phrases like "who died" or "who perished." This kind of direct moral reckoning was, and still is, rare in history.
👨‍👩‍👧 How many Jewish people lived in Berlin when Hitler came to power? ▾
More than 160,000 Jewish people called Berlin home when Hitler took power in 1933. Tens of thousands managed to flee. Many others were arrested, sent to Sachsenhausen concentration camp nearby, and eventually murdered. The memorial remembers them and the six million Jews killed across Europe during the Holocaust.

Potsdamer Platz

Before World War II, Potsdamer Platz was the "Times Square of Berlin" — possibly the busiest square in all of Europe. It was pulverized in the war, then left a deserted no-man's-land for 40 years, cut in two by the Berlin Wall at the intersection of the American, British, and Soviet sectors. Two subtle lines in the pavement still mark where the Wall once ran. With reunification, a massive redevelopment campaign transformed it overnight — Sony, Daimler, and Deutsche Bahn built gleaming skyscrapers here, turning the square into a modern city center once again.

🕐 Spot the Clock: The small green clock tower is a replica of the first electronic traffic light in Europe, which once stood at this very intersection. Also look for the slanted glass cylinders sticking out of the ground — mirrors on their tops rotate with the sun to collect daylight and send it down into the underground train station below.
🎸 What concert marked the start of Potsdamer Platz's rebirth? ▾
In 1990, Pink Floyd performed The Wall right here — a concert that doubled as a symbol of Berlin's reunification and the square's rebirth. The show attracted hundreds of thousands of people to what was still largely a wasteland. Within a decade, it had become one of Europe's most impressive modern urban developments.
🏙️ What are the notable buildings around the square? ▾
The curved, green-glass crescent is the DB Tower — headquarters of Deutsche Bahn, Germany's national railway. To its right are the twin blocky-gray Beisheim Center towers, designed to evoke 1920s Chicago skyscrapers. To the left is the 25-story red-brick Potsdamer Platz 1 building — which looks like a 1920s New York Art Deco tower but was built in 1999. The glass-wedge Forum Tower was designed by renowned Italian architect Renzo Piano, who created the master plan for the entire Potsdamer Platz renovation.
💰 What does Potsdamer Platz symbolize today? ▾
Like great Christian churches built upon ancient holy sites, Potsdamer Platz — with its corporate logos flying high above what was once the Berlin Wall — trumpets the triumph of capitalism over communism. Where barbed wire and guard towers once stood, Sony and Daimler now have their headquarters. It's one of the most dramatic urban transformations in modern European history.

Unter den Linden

Berlin's grandest boulevard — the "Champs-Élysées of Berlin" — stretches east from the Brandenburg Gate through the heart of imperial Germany. The name means "under the linden trees," and the street has been the city's ceremonial spine since the 15th century, when it connected the royal palace to the king's hunting grounds.

🌳 Walk the Boulevard: During Berlin's golden age in the late 1800s, this was one of Europe's most glamorous promenades. After World War II it became the main street of communist East Berlin — so the same street has played starring roles in Prussian splendor, Nazi pageantry, and Soviet-style communism.
👑 Who built most of the grand buildings here? ▾
Frederick the Great, who ruled Prussia from 1740 to 1786, is responsible for most of the landmark buildings along Unter den Linden. Aristocrats built their palaces here to be close to the king. Frederick believed that grand architecture, opera, and education were essential to a civilized society — so he funded institutions like the state library, the opera house, and Humboldt University, all visible along this boulevard.
🪨 What are the bronze plaques embedded in the cobblestones? ▾
In front of Humboldt University's main gate, look down for a row of square bronze plaques — Stolpersteine, or "stumbling stones." Each one bears the name of a university student who was executed by the Nazis. Similar plaques appear all over Berlin, embedded in the pavement in front of the last homes of Holocaust victims. They're meant to make you literally stumble upon history.

Neue Wache (New Guardhouse)

Built in 1816 as a fancy barracks for royal bodyguards, this Neoclassical building — modeled on a Greek temple — has been transformed over the years into Germany's central memorial for victims of war and tyranny. Step inside to find a deeply moving sculpture at its heart: a replica of Käthe Kollwitz's Mother with Her Dead Son.

☁️ Look Up: The memorial is open to the sky — rain, snow, and sunshine all fall directly onto the sculpture. This is intentional. The elements share in the grief. The inscription reads: "To the victims of war and tyranny." Beneath the floor lie the tombs of Germany's unknown soldier and an unknown concentration camp victim.
🎨 Who was Käthe Kollwitz, and why was she chosen? ▾
Käthe Kollwitz was a Berlin artist who lived through both world wars and lost her own son in World War I. Her work focused on working-class suffering, grief, and the human cost of war. She created the original sculpture — Mother with Her Dead Son — as a deeply personal expression of loss. When Germany needed a central war memorial, her image of universal grief was the perfect choice.
⚖️ Why is it complicated to honor German war dead? ▾
Germany started — and lost — both World War I and World War II. Honoring soldiers who fought for the Nazi regime is deeply fraught. The Neue Wache navigates this by focusing on victims of war and tyranny broadly — both German soldiers and victims of Hitler's regime, including concentration camp victims. The inscription avoids glorifying war, instead mourning its costs on all sides.

Museum Island & Berlin Cathedral

Berlin was born on this marshy island in the Spree River around the year 1200. For centuries it was home to the Prussian royal palace. Today it holds five world-class museums — the Pergamon Museum (ancient Greek and Middle Eastern antiquities), the Neues Museum (Egyptian collection including the bust of Nefertiti), the Alte Nationalgalerie (19th-century European art), the Bode Museum (Byzantine art and sculpture), and the Altes Museum (classical antiquities). Dominating the island is the spectacular Berlin Cathedral, built by Kaiser Wilhelm II in an over-the-top mix of Baroque and Renaissance styles dripping with gold mosaics. The cathedral's crypt holds the tombs of Hohenzollern royalty, and its dome can be climbed for panoramic city views.

🏺 Did You Know: The Pergamon Museum houses a full-scale reconstruction of the Pergamon Altar — a 2,000-year-old Greek monument so massive they built the museum around it. You can also walk through the ancient Ishtar Gate of Babylon, originally built by King Nebuchadnezzar II in 575 BC.
🏰 What happened to the original palace that stood here? ▾
The original Stadtschloss was considered the most important secular Baroque building north of the Alps. Heavily damaged in World War II, it was controversially demolished by the communist East German government in 1950 and replaced with a boxy parliament building. That too was later torn down. For years the site was a grassy lot — until the palace was faithfully reconstructed as the Humboldt Forum, reopening in 2021.
🌍 What is the Humboldt Forum? ▾
The Humboldt Forum is the reborn royal palace — rebuilt on the outside to look exactly as it did before World War II, but filled inside with a cutting-edge cultural museum. It houses the ethnological collections of Berlin's state museums, showcasing cultures from Africa, Asia, Oceania, and the Americas. Named after the Humboldt brothers — explorer Alexander and educator Wilhelm — it celebrates global knowledge and human curiosity. Entry to the permanent exhibitions is free.
⛪ Why is the Berlin Cathedral so over-the-top? ▾
Kaiser Wilhelm II — the jingoistic emperor in the spiked helmet who led Europe into World War I — built the cathedral at the turn of the 20th century to project imperial grandeur. The result is a garish mix of Neoclassical, Neo-Baroque, and Neo-Renaissance styles, with rippling stucco and gold-tiled mosaics. It's most impressive from the outside.

Bebelplatz: Book Burning Memorial

Look down through a small glass window set into the cobblestones and you'll see a haunting underground room of empty white bookshelves. On May 10, 1933, Nazis burned 20,000 books here — works by Einstein, Freud, Hemingway, and others. Hitler chose this square, built by Frederick the Great to celebrate culture, to symbolize the end of tolerance.

📚 Think About This: Books by some of the world's greatest minds — scientists, novelists, doctors — were thrown into a fire because the Nazis found the ideas dangerous. The German poet Heinrich Heine wrote in 1820: "Where they burn books, they will ultimately burn people also."
📖 Who were the students who built the bonfire? ▾
The bonfire was built by staff and students from Humboldt University — one of Europe's most prestigious institutions — right across the street. Nazi propaganda minister Joseph Goebbels oversaw the event. This is a sobering reminder that even highly educated people can be drawn into terrible movements. Embedded in the cobblestones nearby are bronze plaques bearing the names of university students executed by the Nazis.

Alexanderplatz & the TV Tower

Alexanderplatz has been Berlin's eastern hub since 1805 — a trade gateway, a roaring 1920s cabaret district, and then the pride of communist East Berlin's commercial center. On November 4, 1989, more than half a million East Berliners flooded this square demanding freedom. Five days later, the Wall fell. Today the square mixes DDR-era buildings with modern shops, anchored by the iconic TV Tower.

😂 Best Joke: The TV Tower was built in 1969 to celebrate communist power — but when sunlight hits its mirrored ball, it creates a glowing cross. East Berliners called it "The Pope's Revenge." They also joked: if it fell over, they'd finally have an elevator to freedom in the West.
✝️ Why did a cross appear on an atheist government's tower? ▾
Communist East Germany was officially atheist and was actively removing religious symbols from churches. But when sunlight hit the TV Tower's mirrored globe, the reflected light created a huge, unmistakable cross. The government tried darkening the glass — the cross kept appearing. Berliners delighted in calling it "The Pope's Revenge."
🕐 What is the World Time Clock? ▾
Standing in Alexanderplatz is the Weltzeituhr — a rotating World Time Clock installed in 1969 that shows the time in cities around the globe. It was a nostalgic symbol of communist East Germany's desire to seem connected to the wider world, even as its citizens were forbidden from traveling there. It remains a beloved meeting point for Berliners today.

The Iron Curtain

The Berlin Wall: A City Divided

On August 13, 1961, East Germans woke up to find barbed wire going up overnight. Within days, a concrete wall followed. For 28 years, families were separated, and an estimated 136 people were killed trying to cross to freedom.

1
The Wall went up overnightOn August 13, 1961, East Germany sealed the border. Residents woke to find barbed wire outside their windows. People leapt from apartment buildings onto waiting rescue nets held by West Berlin firefighters below.
2
Two walls, one death stripThe Wall was actually two parallel walls with a 30–160 foot "death strip" between them: barbed wire, spike strips, patrol dogs, floodlights, and guards with orders to shoot to kill.
3
5,000 people escapedDespite the danger, about 5,000 people made it across — through tunnels, in hidden compartments, across rooftops, even in hot-air balloons. Remarkably, 565 of those who escaped were East German border guards themselves.
4
November 9, 1989: The Wall fallsAn East German spokesman accidentally announced that new travel rules would take effect "immediately." Crowds swarmed the checkpoints. Stunned guards let them through. The Wall was torn down with hammers and bare hands that same night.

800 Years in Brief

A History of Berlin

From a marshy trading post to the capital of a reunified nation — Berlin's story is one of ambition, catastrophe, resilience, and rebirth. Here's the sweep of history your family will feel in every corner of the city.

~1200
Berlin Is Born
A humble trading settlement grows on a marshy island in the Spree River. It's an unpromising start for what will become one of Europe's great capitals.
1400s
Hohenzollern Princes Take Over
The Hohenzollern dynasty makes Berlin their capital. Over the next four centuries, they transform it from a regional backwater into the seat of a powerful European kingdom.
1701
Kingdom of Prussia
Berlin becomes capital of the newly declared Kingdom of Prussia — a rising military and cultural power that will reshape European history.
1740–1786
Frederick the Great's Golden Age
Frederick the Great — a military genius and lover of the arts — puts Prussia on the map. He builds Unter den Linden's grand institutions, promotes religious tolerance, and turns Berlin into a cultural capital. Most of the great landmarks you'll see were built or inspired by him.
1871
United Germany — Berlin as Capital
Germany unifies as a modern nation and Berlin is its natural capital. The city booms with industrialization, quadrupling its population in just 40 years.
1918–1933
Weimar Republic & the Roaring Twenties
After Germany's humiliating defeat in World War I, Berlin thrives as a wild, creative, anything-goes cultural capital. Jazz clubs, cabarets, and avant-garde art flourish — until the Great Depression, unemployment, and political chaos open the door to Adolf Hitler.
1933–1945
Hitler's Berlin
Berlin becomes the headquarters of the Nazi regime. Book burnings, persecution of Jews and minorities, and the machinery of the Holocaust are organized from here. The city suffers devastating Allied bombing raids. Hitler dies in a bunker beneath the city as Soviet troops take control in May 1945.
1945–1961
A City Divided
Berlin is split into four Allied sectors. The American, British, and French sectors become West Berlin; the Soviet sector becomes East Berlin. In 1948–49, the Soviets blockade West Berlin, but an Allied airlift keeps 2.2 million residents fed and supplied for nearly a year. Three million East Germans flee west before the Wall goes up.
1961
The Wall Goes Up — Overnight
On August 13, 1961, East Germany seals the border. The 96-mile Berlin Wall encircles all of West Berlin, turning it into an island of democracy inside communist East Germany. Families are split apart in a single night.
1989
The Wall Comes Down
On November 9, 1989 — one of history's most joyful nights — the Berlin Wall falls. Berliners from east and west embrace each other, climb the Wall, and chip it apart with hammers. A year later, East and West Germany are reunified as one nation.
Today
A New Berlin
More than 30 years after reunification, Berlin is a dynamic, creative, and welcoming world capital — ready to share its extraordinary story with every visitor. The scars remain, but so does an irrepressible spirit.

Life Under Communism

The Trabant: East Germany's Beloved Rattletrap

Communist East Germany (a.k.a. the DDR) is forever linked to its trademark automobile — the Trabant, or, affectionately, the Trabi (TRAH-bee).

Built in Zwickau, Saxony, starting in 1957, the Trabi was the DDR's big play to compete with the popular West German Volkswagen Beetle. But its design and engineering — limited by a centrally planned economy — were nowhere near VW standards. A ride in a Trabi was cramped, bumpy, and smelly.

To gas up your Trabi, you'd open the hood and pour a gas-oil mix into a tank above the two-cylinder, two-stroke engine, which used gravity instead of a fuel pump. The body was made of a recycled, cloth-reinforced plastic resin called Duroplast — so the body was as "green" as the engine was polluting.

On the upside, the construction was so simple that handy East Germans — who, out of necessity, were excellent do-it-yourselfers — could fix just about any problem with a hammer, a screwdriver, and a wrench. Production was limited, so the privilege of owning a Trabi required signing up on a waiting list and waiting many years.

In the summer of 1989, when Hungary suddenly opened its borders, whole fleets of Trabis were left behind by East Germans who went to vacation on Lake Balaton… and decided to flee West, never to come home. The Trabi remained in production until 1991, totaling more than three million rattletraps with lawnmower engines on Eastern Europe's roads.

😂 Trabi Jokes — Who Says Germans Don't Have a Sense of Humor?
  • 🚗 Why is the Trabi the world's quietest car? Because your knees cover your ears.
  • ⛽ How do you double a Trabi's value? Fill the tank.
  • 🪟 "I'd like two windshield wipers for my Trabi." "Sounds like a fair trade."
  • 👷 How many workers does it take to build a Trabi? Three: One to cut, one to fold, and one to paste.
  • 🌡️ Why is the Trabi's back window heated? To keep your hands warm while pushing.
  • 🏎️ When does a Trabi reach top speed? When it's being towed.
  • 💨 What's the longest car on the market? The Trabi, of course, at 60 feet long — 6 feet of car and 54 feet of smoke.

People Who Shaped Berlin

Key Figures in Berlin's Story

Understanding these individuals helps bring Berlin's history to life — from visionary rulers to ordinary people who changed history with their courage.

👑
Frederick the Great
1712 – 1786
Prussia's most celebrated king — a brilliant military tactician and passionate arts lover who played flute, wrote poetry, and built Berlin's grandest institutions. His buildings line Unter den Linden to this day. He promoted religious tolerance and enlightened values... while also waging brutal wars.
⚔️
Napoleon Bonaparte
1769 – 1821
Conquered Berlin in 1806 and stole the famous Goddess of Victory statue from atop the Brandenburg Gate, carting it to Paris. The Prussians got it back when they helped defeat Napoleon in 1813 — and renamed her the Goddess of Victory to celebrate their triumph.
🎨
Käthe Kollwitz
1867 – 1945
A Berlin artist who lived through both world wars, losing her son in World War I. Her powerful sculpture Mother with Her Dead Son — a replica of which sits in the Neue Wache memorial — captures the grief of a nation. Her work speaks directly to the human cost of war.
🏗️
Norman Foster
born 1935
British architect who renovated the Reichstag and added its iconic glass dome. His design — transparent, open, and allowing visitors to look down into the parliament — became a powerful symbol of Germany's renewed commitment to democracy after decades of totalitarianism.
🧱
Chris Gueffroy
1968 – 1989
The last person killed trying to cross the Berlin Wall. He was just 20 years old, shot through the heart on February 6, 1989. Nine months later, the Wall fell. His white cross stands in the Berlin Wall Victims Memorial near the Reichstag.
✊
Ida Siekmann
1902 – 1961
The very first person to die at the Berlin Wall. On August 22, 1961 — just days after the Wall went up — she jumped from her third-floor apartment on Bernauer Strasse, hoping to reach West Berlin. She didn't survive the fall. Her story is told at the Berlin Wall Memorial.

Did You Know?

Berlin is full of surprising stories. Can you guess the answers before you reveal them?

🌉
How did the Berlin Airlift save 2.2 million people without firing a single shot?
In 1948, the Soviets blocked all roads and railways into West Berlin, hoping to starve the city into submission. Instead of fighting their way in, the Western Allies flew in everything West Berliners needed — food, coal, medicine — for nearly a year. By the time the Soviets gave up the blockade in 1949, Allied planes had made over 200,000 flights. Not a single shot was fired.
🍞
When JFK said "Ich bin ein Berliner" — did he accidentally say "I am a jelly donut"?
This is one of history's most repeated myths — and it's almost entirely false. Kennedy said exactly what he meant: "I am a Berliner." In German, calling yourself a "Berliner" means you're from Berlin. A "Berliner" can also refer to a type of jelly donut, but no one at the time thought Kennedy was calling himself a pastry. Berliners were moved and electrified by his speech. The "donut" story only became popular decades later.
🪧
What are the tiny brass plaques embedded in Berlin's sidewalks — and why do people polish them?
All over Berlin, small brass squares called Stolpersteine — "stumbling stones" — are embedded in the pavement in front of the last known addresses of Holocaust victims. Each one bears a name, birth year, and fate. They were created by artist Gunter Demnig, who began installing them in 1992. Today there are over 100,000 across Europe. Locals sometimes polish them so the names stay bright and legible — a small, ongoing act of remembrance.
🏛️
Why is the Reichstag (parliament) built to be six feet taller than the Chancellor's office?
It was deliberately designed that way to remind Germany's Chancellor who is really in charge — the people, through their elected representatives in parliament. The Chancellor is powerful, but the Bundestag (parliament) is literally higher. Germany takes this symbolism seriously, especially given the history of Hitler, who swept away parliament and made himself absolute ruler.
