Where to go

Top Sites for Families

Delft is compact enough to walk everywhere, yet packed with enough history, art, and quirky details to fill two wonderful days. Here are the must-sees — all within easy walking distance of each other.

Oude Delft Canal

Oude Delft

The Oude Delft is the town's first and major trading canal, dating to the 11th century. In the past, boats passing the town used this waterway. Barges are now allowed to line the canal from April through September, and today Delft's restaurants use them as sunny outdoor tables — fueling the new economy of tourism, just as barges once fueled the economy of trade. Along this canal you will also find the Gemeenlandshuis at number 167, the headquarters of the water authority responsible for keeping waterways dredged and managing water levels across the region. On the black iron fence at the corner where Boterbrug meets the canal, look for a memorial plaque to Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723), who invented an early microscope and then used it to discover bacteria. He is buried in the Old Church, visible in the distance to the right. Find the smallest house in Delft at number 147.

📐 Find the smallest house of Delft on this street. See if your whole family can fit in front of it for a photo — then try to imagine actually living there.
Historic Buildingtap to expand
Oude Delft 167

Gemeenlandshuis

This ornate building along the Oude Delft canal is the headquarters of the water authority (Gemeenlandshuis), responsible for keeping waterways dredged and managing water levels in towns and polders — the low-lying land reclaimed from the sea. The colorful coats of arms of the various 17th-century water-authority directors decorate the exterior wall. Water levels are a very big deal here. Across the canal, look for a yardstick displaying the red NAP marking — "Normaal Amsterdams Peil," or normal Amsterdam point — which is the average sea level in Amsterdam. The wide-open Dutch countryside, known as polders, is generally three yards below this point. According to the NAP, the point where you stand is above sea level, and the canal beside you is below — meaning you are standing on what was originally an island.

Agathaplein 1

Prinsenhof Museum

This was once the Convent of St. Agatha. William of Orange took refuge here after the King of Spain put a bounty on his head in 1580 for his role in the revolt of the Netherlands. Figuring the convent was a safe place in a safe city, William stayed here until 1584, when an assassin finally killed him. At the base of the stairs, the two bullet holes from his assassination are still visible in the wall. The museum focuses on the life of William of Orange and also displays a fine collection of blue Delftware and an exhibit about Dutch innovation. Across the courtyard, the Prinzenkwartier building houses temporary exhibits, art galleries, and a café. In the middle of the square is a tranquil green park — an old monastic herb garden. The statue there honors William, considered the founder of the Netherlands. In the far-left corner of the courtyard is the Winkeltje Kouwenhoven old-timey candy shop, where sweets are still made the traditional way.

🕵️ Real Crime Scene: The actual bullet holes from a royal assassination are still in the wall at the base of the stairs — more than 400 years later.
🎯 Why did the assassin fail to collect his reward? ▾
The assassin hoped to collect a reward offered by Spanish King Philip II for killing William of Orange. Instead of getting rich, the assassin was caught, tortured, and executed. The two bullet holes remain in the wall at the base of the stairs, exactly where the shots were fired in 1584.
🏮 What are those blue-and-white lampposts in the courtyard? ▾
Three blue-and-white lampposts in the courtyard were made in Delft's Chinese sister city. They serve as reminders of the 400-year relationship between porcelain makers in China and Delft. The only color that could survive the extremely hot fire of the Chinese porcelain technique was blue — which is why Delftware is blue.
⛪ What was this building before William arrived? ▾
This was the Convent of St. Agatha. When the Dutch provinces broke from Spain, they also broke from the Roman Catholic Church. The Dutch became "reformed" and dissolved the Catholic convents and monasteries. Although the Dutch declared their freedom in 1579, a treaty formally ending the war for Dutch independence didn't come until 1648.

Near Oude Delft Canal

Old Church (Oude Kerk)

The Old Church is smaller and less impressive than the New Church, but it has been around longer and feels more lived-in. Construction began in the 13th century, and the spire was added later — it leans visibly because it was built on an unstable foundation over a filled-in canal. Despite that, for 150 years it was the tallest spire in Delft. The names of its ministers going back to 1572 fill the wall on your right as you enter. The 1548 wooden canopied pulpit is considered one of the finest in the Netherlands, with carvings that demonstrate a mastery of perspective. The floor holds the tombs of three famous Delft residents: Admiral Piet Hein (center nave, front), Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (tall pyramid-shaped monument set into a wall at the back of the left nave), and painter Johannes Vermeer. Vermeer's actual tombstone is a simple stone plaque in the floor toward the front of the left nave. A grander Vermeer monument, installed in 2007, is also in the floor of the left transept.

🔍 Spot This: Vermeer's actual tombstone is just a simple floor plaque near the front of the left nave. The grander monument was only installed in 2007. Find both — they're in different spots.
📜 Why is the inside of the church so bare? ▾
Iconoclastic riots in 1566 and 1572 violently destroyed every hint of Roman Catholic imagery inside the church. The interior looks sober and clean because it was deliberately stripped bare by Protestant reformers. When the Dutch provinces broke from Spain, they also broke from the Roman Catholic Church and became "reformed."
🎵 Why is there a children's song about Admiral Piet Hein? ▾
During the Eighty Years' War, Admiral Piet Hein captured a Spanish silver fleet — a stunning achievement that made him a national hero. He became so beloved that he is still the subject of a popular Dutch children's song today. His tomb sits in the center nave at the front of the Old Church.

City Center

The Markt (Market Square)

The rectangular Markt is clearly a city with a rich history and a wealthy past. Its look today was defined in 1536 when lightning struck the New Church spire, starting a fire that destroyed two-thirds of the town. While all the buildings ringing the square have cellars dating from before the fire, what you see above ground in brick was built after 1536. Delft recovered well. In the 17th century — its golden age — this was a thriving market town with an economy built on textiles and breweries. With 200 breweries, the city exported 80% of its beer. Then in the 18th century the economy collapsed: without a river, local harbor, or major roads, Delft was left behind, becoming a sleeping-beauty town preserved in its intact medieval structure. The square has never really been renovated, perhaps because it must always be ready, on a day's notice, to host a royal funeral. In the middle of the square stands a statue of Hugo Grotius, born in Delft in 1583.

🍔 Look Up: Look for the building above the Subway sandwich shop where a carved Bible on the corner recalls the first printed Dutch Bible, produced right here in Delft in 1477.
⚖️ Who is Hugo Grotius and why does he have a statue here? ▾
Hugo Grotius was born in Delft in 1583. He was the first to establish international rules of the sea — arguing that all oceans were free-trade territory open to every nation. The English, who claimed dominion over all the sea around Britain, disagreed strongly. The controversy went on for nearly a century, during which the two nations fought a two-year war. The eventual agreement — that the sea within cannonball range of your shore is yours, and any waters beyond that are open to all nations — forms the basis of today's maritime laws.

Markt Square

New Church (Nieuwe Kerk)

Dating to the 14th century, the New Church is the needle around which Delft spins. In 1536 lightning struck its spire, starting the great fire that destroyed two-thirds of the town. Today it holds the most important tombs of Dutch royalty. William of Orange (1533–1584), leader of the Dutch revolt against Spain, is buried here. He would normally have been buried in his family's hometown of Breda, but Spain had occupied the city — so Delft became the resting place of the House of Orange. Both of his statues are inside: one in white marble, reclining peacefully, and one in bronze, seated in full armor. The dog at his feet symbolizes loyalty. Above it, an angel of Fame blows a trumpet — and the entire bronze statue is supported by just one slender ankle. Most of William's descendants of the House of Orange are also buried here.

🧗 Challenge: Climb 376 steps to the top in a very narrow staircase. On a clear day you can see the towers of The Hague in one direction and Rotterdam in the other — one of Europe's more dramatic tower climbs.
🔒 What is under the floor near William's tomb? ▾
A large stone slab near William's tomb (near the transept) marks the entrance to a sprawling underground labyrinth that holds the remains of the Dutch royal family. The crypt is strictly off-limits to anyone unrelated to the royal family. TV monitors in the transept show footage from the last few royal burials held in this church.
🟠 Why do Dutch fans wear orange to football matches? ▾
Besides ruling Holland, the Orange family once owned the independent principality of Orange in the south of France. The royal family's official color is orange — which is why the Dutch wear orange to football matches and consider it their national color, despite having a flag that is red, white, and blue.
Historic Buildingtap to expand
Markt Square

Stadhuis (Town Hall)

The Town Hall was rebuilt in 1620 in the Renaissance style after a fire destroyed an earlier building. It's a law court, with Lady Justice and her scales prominently positioned on the facade. Of the 17 states in the Spanish Netherlands, seven seceded and created the Dutch Republic — the United Provinces of the Netherlands. Holland was one of these; above Lady J, look for the coat of arms with the red lion. The independent Netherlands were dominated by Holland (which contained Amsterdam and drove 85 percent of the country's economy). The lower banner (under the stone canopy, above the door) is Delft's — it features a canal, which is what "Delft" means. The sturdy stone tower at the core of the building was originally a prison. Because it needed to be built of heavy stone, builders chose this particular spot — the most geologically stable clay foundation in this naturally marshy city — and the town hall simply grew up around it.

🦁 Look Up: Find the red lion coat of arms above Lady Justice — Holland's symbol. Then look for the canal on Delft's own crest beneath it. Two symbols, two stories, one facade.

Behind the Town Hall

The Waag

The Waag was the city's medieval trading center — the weighing or customs house, with workspaces for goldsmiths and silversmiths upstairs. Step inside (now a fun bar and restaurant) to see its huge timbers and part of the massive old scales. At the end of the street to the far right of the Waag — marked "de centrale" — is the Vleeshal, or meat market, decorated with cow heads above its doors. Fish was traded in the Visbanken, to the left of the meat market. Walk around behind the Waag and look down below — that is the water gate where produce was off-loaded from boats to be weighed and taxed.

🐄 Look for the Cows: The old Vleeshal meat market at the end of the street still has carved cow heads above its doors.

Between the Waag and Oude Delft Canal

Boterbrug (Butter Bridge)

Boterbrug — literally "Butter Bridge" — is a wide street that was once the approach canal (now covered over) leading to the Waag customs house. Merchants knew the drill for all their goods: before you traded, first you weighed, then you paid. Watch out for bikes as you walk here. During Spanish rule, taxation got so out of hand that the Dutch revolted — much as high British taxes angered American colonists during the same period. Boterbrug leads one block to the Oude Delft canal, the town's first and major trading canal. On the black iron fence at the corner is a memorial plaque to Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723), who invented an early microscope and then used it to discover bacteria. He is buried in the Old Church, visible in the distance to the right.

United States History Connection: During Spanish rule, taxation got so out of hand that the Dutch revolted — much as high British taxes angered American colonists during the same period.
💰 How did taxation here lead to revolution? ▾
Merchants passing through Delft had to weigh their cargo and pay a tax at the Waag before they could trade. During Spanish rule, taxation grew so extreme that the Dutch revolted — just as high British taxes sparked the American Revolution. The revolt, led by William of Orange, eventually led to Dutch independence, though a formal peace treaty wasn't signed until 1648.
🌊 Why is this street named a "bridge" when it's not a bridge? ▾
Boterbrug was originally an approach canal — a waterway leading boats directly to the Waag customs house. The canal was later filled in and paved over, becoming the wide street you walk on today. The name "Butter Bridge" is a memory of when goods arrived here by water.
Museumtap to expand
Voldersgracht 21

Vermeer Museum (Vermeer Centrum)

The great Dutch golden age painter Johannes Vermeer (1632–1675) was born in Delft, grew up near the Markt, and set a number of his paintings here. His father, an art dealer, gave Johannes a passion for painting. Late in the artist's career, as Holland fought draining wars against England, the demand for art and luxuries went sour in the Netherlands, forcing Vermeer to downsize — he sold his big home, packed up his wife and 14 children, and moved in with his mother-in-law. He died two years later. Vermeer painted some 37 surviving works. Although Vermeer painted landscapes and scenes from mythology and the Bible, he specialized in depicting the everyday actions of regular people. And though his scenes are usually still and peaceful, he artfully conveys deep tension and suggests a complicated story with subtle body language or the inclusion of a small item — a letter that seems pregnant with significance. Vermeer was also a master of light, capturing it with an artistry that would make the Impressionists jealous two centuries later. After centuries of relative obscurity, Vermeer and his paintings are now appreciated worldwide. Delft owns none of his works — you'll have to visit Amsterdam's Rijksmuseum, with four masterpieces, or The Hague's Mauritshuis museum. The town's Vermeer Center pays tribute to this great artist and his talent. Outside the Old Church, under the trees across from the entrance, you will find a rotating photo cube recalling the painter and his connection to Delft. Inside the Old Church, Vermeer's actual tombstone is a simple stone plaque in the floor toward the front of the left nave. A grander Vermeer monument, reflecting his greater popularity now than in previous generations, was installed in 2007 in the floor of the left transept across from the pulpit.

Rotterdamseweg 196

Royal Delft Museum

The Delft Blue earthenware made at this factory — known in Dutch as the Koninklijke Porceleyne Fles — is famous worldwide, making this the biggest tourist attraction in town. The Dutch East India Company, partly headquartered in Delft, imported many exotic goods from the Far East, including Chinese porcelain. The Chinese designs — including flowers and peacocks — were copied by many local potters. More than three centuries later, their descendants are still going strong, and you can watch them at work in this factory — the only one left of an original 32. To earn the "Royal" designation in the Netherlands, a company must be at least 100 years old. This one began operating in 1653. You can see painters trace or freehand traditional decorations onto the pottery using a black paint containing cobalt and copper oxide — the biscuit immediately soaks up the paint, making it a very unforgiving medium for mistakes. A second firing transforms the black paint into the famous Delft Blue.

🧱 Why isn't Dutch clay used to make Delftware? ▾
The clay used for Delftware is mainly imported from England and Germany. Below-sea-level Holland only has what is called "dirty clay," which is used to make bricks. The fine clay needed for pottery must come from abroad.

Through the Ages

Delft's Dramatic History

From a marshy village to a golden-age powerhouse, Delft has seen fire, revolution, royal assassination, and artistic genius. Here's the story.

11th C
The Canal Is Dug

The Oude Delft canal — the oldest in the city — is dug, and Delft begins to take shape as a trading settlement. "Delft" means canal in Dutch. Built on marshy lowlands, the city's builders always had to be clever about foundations.

1477
First Printed Dutch Bible

The first Dutch-language Bible is printed right here in Delft, at what is now #57 on the Markt. A carved Bible on the building's upstairs corner still marks the spot today — directly above a Subway sandwich shop.

1536
The Great Fire

Lightning strikes the spire of the New Church, igniting a fire that destroys two-thirds of the town. Delft is rebuilt in brick. Everything you see above ground on the Markt dates from after this catastrophe, though ancient cellars survived underground.

1579–1648
The Dutch Revolt

Seven of the 17 Spanish Netherlands provinces break away to form the Dutch Republic. William of Orange leads the revolt from Delft after Spain puts a bounty on his head. Heavy taxation — the same spark that later ignited the American Revolution — fuels the rebellion.

1584
Assassination of William of Orange

William I of Orange, leader of the Dutch revolt, is shot and killed at the Prinsenhof in Delft. His hometown of Breda was occupied by Spain, so he was buried in Delft's New Church — beginning the tradition of Dutch royalty being interred there. The assassin hoped to collect the bounty offered by Spanish King Philip II but was caught, tortured, and executed instead. The bullet holes remain in the wall.

1600s
The Golden Age

Delft thrives with 200 breweries, exporting 80% of its beer. The Dutch East India Company (partly based here) imports Chinese porcelain, inspiring local potters to create Delftware. Vermeer paints his masterpieces. Van Leeuwenhoek invents the microscope and discovers bacteria. This small city punches far above its weight in world history.

1653
Royal Delft Factory Founded

The factory now known as Royal Delft opens — the only one of 32 original Delftware factories to survive to the present day. To earn the "Royal" designation in the Netherlands, a business must operate for at least 100 years.

1700s
Economic Collapse & Sleeping Beauty

Without a river, major harbor, or connecting roads, Delft is left behind as trade shifts. The city becomes a "sleeping beauty" — its medieval structure perfectly preserved, simply awaiting rediscovery. Today that preservation is the reason visiting Delft feels like stepping back in time.

Born or Buried Here

Delft's Famous Residents

For a small city, Delft produced an astonishing list of world-changers. These are the people whose names you'll encounter on every corner.

⚔️
William of Orange
1533 – 1584

Leader of the Dutch revolt against Spanish rule and considered the founder of the Netherlands. Hid from assassins in Delft's Prinsenhof convent and was ultimately killed there. His death began the tradition of Dutch royalty being buried in the New Church. His memorial there features two statues, a loyal dog, and an angel of Fame balanced on a single ankle.

🎨
Johannes Vermeer
1632 – 1675

One of the greatest painters in history — famous for works like "Girl with a Pearl Earring." Born, lived, and buried right here in Delft. During his lifetime he wasn't especially famous; a grander monument was only installed in the Old Church floor in 2007, reflecting how much more celebrated he is now than in his own time.

🔬
Antonie van Leeuwenhoek
1632 – 1723

Born the same year as Vermeer! Van Leeuwenhoek invented an early microscope powerful enough to discover bacteria — forever changing medicine and biology. He's buried in the Old Church and commemorated by a plaque on the iron fence along Oude Delft. Without him, modern medicine as we know it would be unimaginable.

⚖️
Hugo Grotius
1583 – 1645

Born in Delft, Grotius was the first to argue that the world's oceans should be free-trade territory open to all nations. The English disagreed so strongly they went to war over it. The eventual compromise — your waters extend to cannonball range from shore; everything beyond is open to all — still forms the basis of international maritime law today.

⚓
Admiral Piet Hein
1577 – 1629

A Dutch naval hero buried in the Old Church who, during the Eighty Years' War, captured an entire Spanish silver fleet — one of the greatest naval coups in Dutch history. He became so beloved that he's still the subject of a famous Dutch children's song sung today. His tomb is in the center nave at the front of the Old Church.

The Famous Blue Pottery

How Delftware Is Made

The Dutch East India Company brought Chinese porcelain to the Netherlands, and Delft's potters were so inspired — and competitive — they learned to make their own version. The only color that could survive the intense heat of the Chinese technique was blue. That's why Delftware is blue. Here's how it's still made today at the Royal Delft factory.

🧪 Science Fact: Artists paint with black paint containing cobalt and copper oxide. A chemical reaction in the kiln transforms the black into brilliant blue — and the opaque white glaze melts into a transparent, glass-like outer shell.
1
Mix the ClayClay imported from England and Germany is mixed with water into a liquid slurry. Dutch clay is too "dirty" for fine pottery — it's only good for bricks!
2
Shape ItPlates: liquid clay is spun on a disk like a Dutch pancake and pressed into plate molds. Vases and figurines: liquid clay is poured into hollow plaster molds. The plaster absorbs the water, leaving a clay shell of the right thickness.
3
First FiringThe dry clay object goes into the kiln, where it transforms from gray to white. The result is called "biscuit" — unfired, unglazed, and undecorated.
4
Paint the DesignArtists paint the biscuit using black paint made with cobalt and copper oxide, applied with sable-hair brushes. The porous biscuit soaks up paint immediately — there is absolutely no erasing mistakes!
5
Glaze & Second FiringThe piece is dipped in opaque white glaze, then fired again. Heat triggers the chemical reaction: black becomes brilliant blue, and the glaze melts into a transparent glass-like shell. The famous Delft Blue is born.
