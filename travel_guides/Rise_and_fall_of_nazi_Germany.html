Pre-1919
Origins: War, Collapse, and the Seeds of Extremism

The forces that gave rise to Nazism were rooted in the catastrophe of the First World War. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in June 1914 triggered the conflict that became the most devastating war Europe had ever seen. By the time the ceasefire took effect on November 11, 1918, empires had collapsed, millions were dead, and the continent was reshaping itself under enormous stress.

Germany's defeat was a trauma that many could not accept. Field Marshal Paul von Hindenburg — who would later play a crucial role in Hitler's rise — helped spread the myth that Germany's military had been "stabbed in the back" by traitors at home rather than defeated on the battlefield. This lie proved enormously useful to extreme nationalist movements in the years that followed.

Antisemitism was not invented by the Nazis — it had deep roots in European history. In the Middle Ages, prejudice against Jews was primarily religious. By the 18th and 19th centuries, new forms of economic, nationalist, and racial antisemitism emerged. By the early 20th century, pseudoscientific theories of race and eugenics provided a new and dangerous vocabulary for hatred. Nazi ideology drew upon all of these strands, especially racial antisemitism — the idea that Jews formed a separate and inferior race threatening the German people.

1919–1932
The Weimar Republic and the Rise of the Nazi Party

In the postwar environment, the Weimar Republic — Germany's new parliamentary democracy — struggled from its birth. It was burdened by war guilt, economic disaster, political extremism from both left and right, and the humiliating terms of the Treaty of Versailles, which forced Germany to accept sole responsibility for starting the war and pay enormous reparations.

In this atmosphere, Adolf Hitler emerged as the leader of the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP), known as the Nazi Party. In February 1920, Hitler presented a 25-point party platform. In September 1919, he had already written his first formal statement laying out his views on the so-called "Jewish Question." His ideology combined extreme German nationalism, virulent antisemitism, and hostility to democracy.

The Nazis and other extreme nationalist groups grew in this chaos as visible threats to Germany's fragile democracy. The instability of the Weimar Republic, fear of communism, and the economic shocks of the Great Depression — which hit Germany with devastating force after the US stock market crashed in October 1929 — made millions of Germans more receptive to Nazi ideas.

1933
Seizure of Power: Democracy Dismantled

On January 30, 1933, President Paul von Hindenburg appointed Adolf Hitler as Chancellor of Germany. The Nazi Party assumed control of the German state. This date marks the beginning of the Holocaust era. Within weeks, the Nazis began systematically destroying German democracy and constructing a dictatorship.

In February 1933, after the Reichstag building burned under suspicious circumstances, President Hindenburg signed the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending constitutional protections throughout Germany. By August 1934, following Hindenburg's death, Hitler merged the offices of president and chancellor into one, becoming Germany's absolute dictator. A referendum confirmed his new title and powers.

1933–1938
Consolidation of the Dictatorship and Persecution of Jews

During the first six years of Nazi rule, the regime implemented sweeping discrimination against Jews, transforming them from citizens into outcasts under the law. Laws excluded Jews from civil service, journalism, and public schools. Jewish passports were stamped with the letter "J." A new law required Jewish men to add "Israel" and women "Sara" to their names, marking them publicly. All Jewish-owned businesses were eventually shut down.

The Nazis also targeted other groups they deemed enemies of the German state: political opponents were imprisoned in concentration camps; men accused of homosexuality were targeted under a revised Paragraph 175; Jehovah's Witnesses were banned and imprisoned; people with disabilities were forcibly sterilized under the Law for the Prevention of Offspring with Hereditary Diseases; and Roma and Sinti people faced roundup and confinement. In December 1938, Himmler ordered that Nazi policy toward Roma and Sinti be developed according to racial principles.

Internationally, the regime pursued an aggressive foreign policy. Germany remilitarized, signed the Anglo-German Naval Agreement to expand its navy beyond Versailles limits, and annexed Austria in March 1938 (the Anschluss). Britain and France, following a policy of appeasement championed by Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain, conceded the Sudetenland to Germany at the Munich Conference in September 1938. Winston Churchill, then a member of Parliament, condemned the agreement as a "disaster of the first magnitude."

1939–1941
World War II and the Escalation of Mass Killing

On January 30, 1939, Hitler gave a speech to the Reichstag in which he declared that if war broke out, it would mean the destruction of European Jewry. His words were not metaphor. On September 1, 1939, Germany invaded Poland, unleashing World War II in Europe. Britain and France declared war on Germany two days later.

The war radicalized Nazi policy at every level. Germany rapidly conquered Poland, Denmark, Norway, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, France, Yugoslavia, and Greece. Despite fierce air battle during the Battle of Britain — during which Winston Churchill became Prime Minister — Germany failed to defeat Britain. In June 1941, Germany launched Operation Barbarossa, its massive invasion of the Soviet Union, which Hitler described as a racial war of annihilation against what he called "Jewish-Bolshevism."

The invasion of the Soviet Union triggered immediate mass murder. Einsatzgruppen — special SS task forces — followed the advancing German army, rounding up and shooting Jews en masse. At first they targeted Jewish men of military age; by August 1941 they were massacring entire communities — men, women, and children — in broad daylight. A massacre near Kamenets-Podolsk in August 1941 killed 23,600 Jews. In October 1941, 9,200 residents of the Kovno Ghetto were massacred at Fort IX.

In occupied Poland, German authorities established ghettos where Jews were confined under miserable, overcrowded conditions. The Warsaw Ghetto — sealed in November 1940 behind a wall topped with barbed wire — imprisoned over 400,000 Jews. Starvation, disease, and arbitrary violence killed hundreds of thousands. Similar ghettos were established in Kraków, Kovno, Łódź, and dozens of other cities.

Inside Germany and occupied Polish territory, a separate program of murder was underway. The so-called Euthanasia Program — authorized by Hitler in a backdated order to the start of the war — targeted people with disabilities. German physicians and SS staff used specially designed gas chambers to kill institutionalized patients at facilities including Hartheim Castle. This program also served as a testing ground for gassing technology that would later be used in the killing centers.

1942–1944
The "Final Solution": Systematic Genocide

On January 20, 1942, senior Nazi officials convened at a villa in Wannsee, Berlin. Reinhard Heydrich, chief of the Reich Security Main Office, chaired the meeting and presented plans to coordinate a European-wide "Final Solution of the Jewish Question" — the deliberate and systematic mass murder of all of Europe's Jews. The Wannsee Conference did not initiate the killing, which had already begun, but organized and expanded its bureaucratic machinery across the entire continent.

To implement the Final Solution in occupied Poland, the SS established five dedicated killing centers: Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, and Auschwitz-Birkenau. Gassing operations began at Treblinka in July 1942. Jews were transported from ghettos and from across occupied Europe to these facilities by rail, told they were being "resettled." The vast majority were gassed almost immediately upon arrival. Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest killing center, became the site of the murder of over a million Jews alone.

German authorities, with the help of allies and collaborators, deported Jews from across the continent: from France, the Netherlands (beginning July 1942), Norway (October 1942), Greece, Italy, and many other countries. In May–July 1944, Hungarian gendarmerie officials, under German SS guidance, deported some 440,000 Jews from Hungary to Auschwitz-Birkenau — one of the largest and fastest deportation operations of the entire Holocaust.

As the killings intensified, prisoners in some camps staged armed revolts. In October 1943, Jewish prisoners at the Sobibór killing center carried out an armed uprising. In October 1944, prisoners assigned to the crematorium at Auschwitz-Birkenau revolted after learning they were to be killed. In November 1944, at Heinrich Himmler's order, the Auschwitz authorities began demolishing the gas chambers and crematoria to hide the evidence of genocide as Soviet forces advanced.

Elsewhere, small numbers of individual rescuers risked their lives to protect Jews. In September 1943, approximately 7,200 Danish Jews were secretly ferried by boat to neutral Sweden. Swedish diplomat Raoul Wallenberg arrived in Budapest in July 1944 and worked to protect Jews by issuing protective Swedish documents. The Allied nations formally acknowledged the mass murder in a joint declaration in December 1942.

Resistance
Jewish Resistance and Acts of Rescue

Jewish individuals and communities resisted Nazi persecution in many ways — both armed and unarmed — despite facing overwhelming force and near-impossible odds. The most famous example of armed resistance is the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943. When German forces entered the ghetto to deport its remaining residents, vastly outnumbered and outgunned fighters — many affiliated with underground organizations — hid and fought the Germans for almost a month before being crushed.

Notable Acts of Resistance and Rescue

The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (April 19–May 1943) — led in part by Mordecai Anielewicz of the Jewish Fighting Organization — became the most prominent symbol of armed Jewish resistance. The Sobibór Uprising (October 14, 1943) saw Jewish prisoners kill several SS guards before escaping. The Prisoner Revolt at Auschwitz-Birkenau (October 7, 1944) resulted in the destruction of one of the crematoria. In Denmark, ordinary citizens organized the rescue of approximately 7,200 Jews. Diplomat Raoul Wallenberg saved tens of thousands of Hungarian Jews through Swedish protective documents. Emanuel Ringelblum organized the Oneg Shabbat archive in the Warsaw Ghetto, secretly documenting life under occupation to preserve a historical record of the atrocities.

1944–1945
Military Defeat and the Liberation of the Camps

Even as the Holocaust reached its most murderous phase in 1944, Germany's military position was collapsing. On June 6, 1944 — D-Day — American, British, and Canadian forces landed on the beaches of Normandy under Operation Overlord, opening the long-awaited second front in Western Europe. Soviet forces simultaneously pressed westward on the Eastern Front, and Allied forces landed in Italy, liberating Rome in June 1944. By the end of 1944, the Allies had liberated the majority of Axis-occupied territories.

As Soviet forces closed in from the east, the SS began evacuating prisoners from camps in the form of forced marches — so-called "death marches" — in which guards brutally mistreated and killed many of the prisoners. The largest death marches began from Auschwitz in January 1945.

Soviet forces were first to approach a major Nazi camp, reaching the Majdanek killing center near Lublin in July 1944. They liberated Auschwitz on January 27, 1945, finding approximately 7,000 prisoners — most ill and dying. US forces liberated camps including Ohrdruf (April 4), Buchenwald (April 11), and Dachau (April 29). British forces liberated Bergen-Belsen on April 15, discovering some 55,000 survivors in horrifying conditions. On April 30, 1945, as Soviet forces encircled Berlin, Adolf Hitler died by suicide in his underground bunker. Germany surrendered unconditionally — in the west on May 7, and in the east on May 9, 1945.

Aftermath
Justice, Reckoning, and Legacy

By the end of the Holocaust, the Nazi German regime and its allies and collaborators had murdered six million European Jews — nearly two out of every three European Jews alive in 1933. Millions of others were also murdered, including Soviet prisoners of war, Polish civilians, Roma and Sinti, people with disabilities, and political opponents. Thousands of Jewish communities across Europe were devastated or completely destroyed.

Many survivors faced continued violence even after liberation. In July 1946, a pogrom in Kielce, Poland, killed dozens of Jewish survivors — demonstrating that antisemitism had not ended with Germany's defeat. Hundreds of thousands of Jewish displaced persons waited in camps, often for years, before immigrating to new homes in what became the State of Israel, the United States (under the Displaced Persons Act of 1948), or elsewhere.

The effort to hold perpetrators accountable began almost immediately. On November 20, 1945, the International Military Tribunal opened in Nuremberg, Germany, trying 21 major Nazi leaders. American Chief Prosecutor Justice Robert Jackson delivered a landmark opening statement. On October 1, 1946, the tribunal delivered its verdicts: 12 defendants were sentenced to death, including Joachim von Ribbentrop and Hans Frank; others received prison sentences. Hermann Göring — sentenced to death — died by suicide before his execution. Adolf Eichmann evaded capture for years, but was caught, tried in Israel, found guilty of crimes against the Jewish people, and executed in 1962.

The Holocaust also gave rise to new legal frameworks to prevent future genocides. The scholar Raphael Lemkin — who had coined the word "genocide" — campaigned to have it recognized under international law. The UN Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of Genocide entered into force in January 1951.
